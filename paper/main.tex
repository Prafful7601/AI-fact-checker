\title{Certified Numeric Verification: Guaranteed-Error Fact-Checking of\\ Numeric Claims over Tables with Automated Human Escalation}

\author{Prafful Gupta, Prashant Kumar Singh\\
\textit{Department of Computer Applications (MCA), KIET Group of Institutions}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models verify numeric claims about tables unreliably: they
miscount rows, mis-sum columns, and get comparisons wrong, yet the sentences
they produce for a wrong numeric claim are nearly indistinguishable, in
meaning, from the sentence for the correct one. Citation-based fact-checking
does not help here, because aggregated numbers -- counts, totals, averages --
are not written anywhere in the source table to cite. We present Certified
Numeric Verification (CNV), a system that (1) introduces
NumNear-TabFact, a large, programmatically constructed and label-flip-verified
benchmark of minimally perturbed numeric near-miss claims built from
TabFact, showing that such near-misses have a mean cosine similarity of
$0.961$ (91.2\% of pairs above $0.9$) to their true counterparts under a
standard sentence embedding model while being factually opposite; (2)
replaces free-text LLM verdicts with \emph{execution-agreement confidence}:
an LLM translates a claim into an executable check over the table, sampled
$K$ times, and confidence is the agreement among the $K$ executed verdicts;
and (3) applies split-conformal risk control to that confidence so that,
among the claims the system answers, the error rate is guaranteed, with high
probability, not to exceed a chosen $\alpha$ -- every claim below threshold
is instead routed to a human reviewer through an automated RPA workflow. On
8{,}205 TabFact and NumNear-TabFact test claims evaluated with a hosted LLM
(Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite), we find that within-method execution-agreement
alone, while positively correlated with correctness, is too coarse-grained
(only $K{+}1$ distinguishable levels) to certify any of the strict error
rates $\alpha\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$ with non-trivial coverage; however, a
cross-method signal -- agreement between the executed-check verdict and an
independently-elicited free-text verdict -- does certify $\alpha=0.10$ at
$43.9\%$ coverage. A secondary run on a small local open-weight model
(Qwen2.5-7B, 3{,}752 claims to date) shows the same qualitative pattern at
lower absolute accuracy, indicating the approach's reliability scales with
base-model capability. Our contribution targets SDG 16 (public access to
reliable information) and, as an institutional deployment, SDG 4
(educational information).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to answer factual
questions about tabular data -- fee structures, exam results, budgets,
schedules -- yet they remain unreliable at exactly the kind of reasoning
tables demand: counting rows, summing a column, computing an average,
comparing two cells. A claim like ``the total fee for MCA semester~1 is
95{,}000'' and the claim ``the total fee for MCA semester~1 is 99{,}000''
differ by a single number, but one is true and one is false. An LLM asked to
verify either claim in free text can silently get the arithmetic wrong and
report high confidence in the wrong answer, with no trace of how it arrived
there.

Citation-based fact-checking, the standard defense against LLM
hallucination, does not solve this problem. A citation points to a passage
that supports a claim; but the number ``95{,}000'' in the example above is
not written anywhere in the source table -- it is the \emph{sum} of a
tuition-fee cell and a hostel-fee cell. There is no sentence to cite for a
count, a total, or an average; the fact must be \emph{computed}, and citing
the table itself does nothing to tell a reader whether the computation was
done correctly. Worse, because a wrong numeric claim is semantically almost
identical to the correct one -- same subject, same units, same sentence
structure, one digit different -- similarity-based and retrieval-based
verifiers, which work by checking whether a claim is entailed by *similar*
evidence, cannot distinguish the two: both claims are equally ``similar'' to
the table.

This near-miss problem is not hypothetical. In
Section~\ref{sec:nearmiss} we construct NumNear-TabFact, a large set of
claims built by changing exactly one number in a true TabFact claim just
enough to flip its label, and show that the average cosine similarity
between a true claim and its near-miss, under a standard sentence embedding
model, is $0.961$, with $91.2\%$ of pairs above $0.9$
similarity. As Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, LLM-only verdicts (B1) do
not degrade uniformly on these near-misses -- accuracy actually rises on
this split, for reasons that turn out to matter for how these results should
be read, not because the model is detecting the perturbation directly. A
system that cannot distinguish these pairs by meaning alone has no choice
but to compute the disputed number directly.

Our approach is to stop asking the LLM to be the arithmetic engine and
instead ask it to be a \emph{translator}: given a claim and a table, the LLM
emits a small, executable ``check'' -- a JSON program with a row filter, a
target column, and an operation such as \texttt{count}, \texttt{sum},
\texttt{avg}, or \texttt{compare} -- and a Python/pandas executor computes
the verdict deterministically. Because LLM outputs are still stochastic, we
sample $K$ such checks per claim and use the \emph{agreement} among their
executed verdicts as a confidence signal: a claim where all $K$ independently
generated, independently executed checks agree is one we can trust more than
a claim where they split. We then calibrate this confidence with
split-conformal risk control (Section~\ref{sec:method}), which lets us fix a
target error rate $\alpha$ in advance -- $\alpha\in\{0.02, 0.05, 0.10\}$ in
our experiments -- and derive an agreement threshold such that, among claims
the system chooses to answer, the empirical error rate is guaranteed, with
high probability under exchangeability, not to exceed $\alpha$. Every claim
below threshold abstains and is routed automatically to a human reviewer
through a UiPath robotic process automation (RPA) workflow, with a
quantified, bounded review workload rather than an ad hoc escalation rule.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 -- NumNear-TabFact.} A large numeric near-miss benchmark
built programmatically (no LLM calls) from TabFact's train and test splits,
by matching a number in a true claim to a uniquely occurring value in its
table (a raw cell or a computed aggregate) and perturbing it -- off-by-one,
digit swap, magnitude change, or round-number change -- while verifying,
against the table, that the label actually flips.
\item \textbf{C2 -- Execution-agreement confidence.} A JSON check language
executed deterministically over the table, sampled $K$ times per claim, with
confidence defined as agreement among the $K$ executed verdicts; invalid or
failing checks count as abstain votes.
\item \textbf{C3 -- Conformal risk control.} Split-conformal calibration of
the agreement threshold so that the error rate among answered claims is
$\leq\alpha$ with high probability, for $\alpha\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$, with
every abstained claim escalated to a human-review queue whose workload we
measure directly.
\end{itemize}

The system is deployed end-to-end: a FastAPI service exposes the verifier to
a UiPath REFramework automation that dispatches claims, calls the API,
routes answered/abstained results, and escalates overdue review tickets by
email (Section~\ref{sec:deployment}), so that the statistical guarantee in
C3 translates into an operational, auditable process rather than a number in
a paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Table fact verification and program-aided reasoning.} TabFact
\cite{chen2020tabfact} introduced verifying statements against Wikipedia
tables and proposed the Latent Program Algorithm, a trained semantic parser
that executes a program against the table. Later work replaced the trained
parser with LLM-driven program synthesis: Binder \cite{cheng2023binder}
binds an LLM inside a SQL/Python program; Dater \cite{ye2023dater} decomposes
tables and questions before self-consistency; Chain-of-Table
\cite{wang2024chainoftable} evolves the table as an intermediate reasoning
state with greedy (non-sampling) search; PAL \cite{gao2023pal} and
Program-of-Thoughts \cite{chen2023pot} offload word-problem arithmetic to a
Python interpreter. All target general program-construction accuracy; none
targets the numeric-near-miss failure mode or couples execution to a
statistical abstention guarantee with a measured human-review cost. Our
check language and $K$-sample agreement are a zero-shot analogue of the
program-execution idea, purpose-built to feed a conformal gate.

\textbf{Numerical perturbation in fact-checking.} NumPert
\cite{aarnes2025numpert} perturbs numbers in open-domain claim/evidence
pairs (accuracy drops up to 62\%), but over free text, with no
table-grounded, label-flip-verified construction. QuanTemp
\cite{venktesh2024quantemp} is a large benchmark of naturally occurring
numerical claims against retrieved web text, not a controlled minimal-pair
or tabular benchmark. NumNear-TabFact is complementary: a controlled,
table-grounded, programmatically label-verified near-miss benchmark isolating
the ``semantically near-identical, numerically wrong'' failure mode for
tables specifically.

\textbf{Conformal prediction and selective prediction for LLMs.} Conformal
prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle} gives distribution-free,
finite-sample coverage guarantees from a calibration set; conformal risk
control \cite{angelopoulos2024risk} generalizes this to monotone risk via a
finite-sample correction, which we use to calibrate our agreement threshold.
Mohri and Hashimoto \cite{mohri2024conformal} bound long-form generation
factuality via a conformal back-off that retracts sub-claims; Abbasi Yadkori
et al.\ \cite{abbasiyadkori2024conformal} use self-consistency and conformal
calibration to bound hallucination rate on open-domain QA. Both target
open-ended generation with LLM-judged similarity as the confidence signal;
neither applies to table-grounded numeric verification or ties abstention to
an executable, auditable check, as our execution-agreement signal does.

\textbf{RPA in institutional administration.} RPA increasingly automates
rule-based administrative workflows in higher education
\cite{bhardwaj2025rpa}, with reported time and error gains for admissions and
reporting. We extend this by using RPA to carry the overflow of a
statistically calibrated abstention decision -- the review queue's size is a
direct, quantified consequence of $\alpha$, not a separately tuned rule.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

Let a \emph{table} $T$ be a finite set of named columns and rows, each cell
holding a string or number. A \emph{claim} $c$ is a natural-language
statement about $T$. A \emph{verdict} $v(c,T)\in\{\textsc{true},
\textsc{false}\}$ is the ground-truth entailment label of $c$ against $T$.
A verification system $f$ produces, for each $(c,T)$, either an answer
$\hat v(c,T)\in\{\textsc{true},\textsc{false}\}$ together with a confidence
$s(c,T)\in[0,1]$, or an explicit \emph{abstention} $\hat v(c,T)=\bot$.

Given a confidence threshold $\lambda$, the system \emph{answers} iff
$s(c,T)\ge\lambda$ and abstains otherwise. The \emph{selective risk} at
$\lambda$ over a distribution $\mathcal D$ of claims is
\begin{equation}
R(\lambda) = \Pr_{(c,T)\sim\mathcal D}\big[\hat v(c,T)\neq v(c,T) \mid
s(c,T)\ge\lambda\big],
\end{equation}
and the \emph{coverage} is $\Pr_{(c,T)\sim\mathcal D}[s(c,T)\ge\lambda]$, the
fraction of claims the system is willing to answer. Given a target error
rate $\alpha\in(0,1)$, our goal (C3) is to choose $\lambda$, using only a
held-out \emph{calibration} split $\mathcal D_{\text{cal}}$ exchangeable
with the test distribution, such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr\big[R(\lambda) \le \alpha\big] \ge 1-\delta
\end{equation}
for a small failure probability $\delta$, while maximizing coverage. This is
the standard split-conformal risk-control guarantee
\cite{angelopoulos2024risk}: it holds marginally over the randomness of the
calibration split (Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the
exchangeability assumption this relies on), not conditionally on any single
claim. Every claim with $s(c,T)<\lambda$ is, by construction, routed to a
human reviewer rather than answered incorrectly by the system.

\subsection{Check language}
A check is a JSON object with (i) a list of \emph{row filters}
$(\text{column}, \text{operator}, \text{value})$ with
$\text{operator}\in\{=,\neq,>,<,\ge,\le,\texttt{contains}\}$; (ii) a
\emph{target column}; (iii) an \emph{operation} in $\{\texttt{lookup},
\texttt{count}, \texttt{sum}, \texttt{avg}, \texttt{max}, \texttt{min},
\texttt{argmax}, \texttt{argmin}, \texttt{compare}, \texttt{difference}\}$;
and (iv) an \emph{expected value} and \emph{comparator} relating it to the
computed result. Tables are serialized to the LLM as markdown with column
headers. Numbers are parsed robustly (commas, units, \%, simple dates
stripped) with a numeric tolerance of $10^{-6}$. A check that fails to parse,
references a nonexistent column, or raises during execution is marked
\textsc{invalid}, never silently promoted to a verdict. The check-generation
prompt gives the exact operator vocabulary and two worked examples (added
after early testing showed operator-syntax mistakes, e.g.\ \texttt{==} for
\texttt{=}, as a recurring failure); the executor also normalizes common
syntactic variants. This prompt is identical for both models evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Execution-agreement confidence (C2)}
For a claim $c$ and table $T$, we sample $K$ checks from the LLM at
temperature $0.7$, execute each independently, and discard \textsc{invalid}
executions (they count as abstain votes, i.e.\ they reduce the agreement
score without being allowed to inflate it). Let $n_T,n_F$ be the number of
valid executed checks voting \textsc{true}/\textsc{false}. The verdict is
the majority vote and the confidence is
$s(c,T) = \max(n_T,n_F)/K$ -- note the denominator is $K$, not the number of
valid votes, so a claim where several samples fail to parse is penalized
exactly as it should be: we have less independent evidence for it. If
$n_T=n_F=0$ (all $K$ checks invalid), the claim is \textsc{unverifiable} and
always abstains regardless of $\lambda$.

\subsection{Cross-method confidence}
\label{sec:cross-method}
$s(c,T)$ above is a \emph{within-method} signal: all $K$ samples come from
the same check-generation prompt, so they can only take $K+1$ distinct
values ($0, 1/K, \ldots, 1$). As Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, this
coarseness caps how selectively the system can filter claims, regardless of
how the conformal threshold is chosen. We therefore also evaluate a
\emph{cross-method} signal that combines the execution-based verdict with an
independently-elicited free-text verdict (B2, Section~\ref{sec:baselines}):
\begin{equation}
s_{\times}(c,T) = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{s(c,T) + s_{B2}(c,T)}{2} & \text{if the two verdicts agree} \\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $s_{B2}$ is B2's self-reported confidence rescaled to $[0,1]$. Two
verdicts reached by different means -- one via code execution, one via
free-text reasoning -- agreeing is a qualitatively different, and as we find
empirically stronger, piece of evidence than either method's internal
self-consistency alone.

\subsection{Conformal calibration (C3)}
We apply the same calibration procedure independently to each of the three
confidence signals above ($s$, $s_{B2}$, and $s_\times$), so that
Section~\ref{sec:results} can report where each one does and does not
certify a usable operating point. Given a calibration split with
$(s_i, \text{correct}_i)_{i=1}^n$ (a confidence score and whether the
corresponding verdict matched the gold label), we select
$\lambda_\alpha$ as the smallest threshold such that the finite-sample
upper confidence bound on selective risk, using the ``add-one'' correction
$\widehat R_{\text{ucb}}(\lambda) = (\text{wrong}(\lambda)+1)/
(\text{answered}(\lambda)+1)$, remains $\le\alpha$ for every threshold at or
above it (a suffix-monotonicity correction over the empirical curve, which
guards against the non-monotonicity a finite sample can introduce). This
follows the conformal risk control framework of
\cite{angelopoulos2024risk}. We report realised risk and coverage on a
disjoint test split, and repeat the calibration/test split 100 times to
verify $\Pr[R(\lambda_\alpha)\le\alpha]$ holds at the target rate
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
\textbf{B1 (LLM-only):} the LLM is shown the table and claim and answers
\textsc{true}/\textsc{false} directly, in free text, with no execution.
\textbf{B2 (LLM-only + self-reported confidence):} as B1, but the LLM also
reports a confidence in $[0,100]$, which we calibrate with the same
conformal procedure as C3 for a fair comparison of confidence sources.
\textbf{B3 (single check, $K=1$):} one sampled check, executed once; an
invalid check yields \textsc{unverifiable} directly, with no agreement
signal (agreement is degenerate at $0$ or $1$).

\section{NumNear-TabFact}
\label{sec:nearmiss}

\subsection{Construction}
NumNear-TabFact is built entirely programmatically (no LLM calls) from true
($\textsc{true}$-labelled) TabFact claims in the train and test splits. For
each candidate claim we build a table's \emph{grounding pool}: every
parseable raw cell value, plus, for each sufficiently numeric column, its
sum, mean, max, and min, and the table's row count (for count-style claims).
A number in the claim is an \emph{anchor} if it matches, within tolerance
$10^{-6}$, exactly one value in the grounding pool -- i.e.\ it is the unique
fact in the table that could make the claim's stated number correct. We then
apply four perturbation operators to the anchor -- \texttt{off\_by\_one},
\texttt{digit\_swap}, \texttt{magnitude\_change} (\'{\i}$\times 10$ or
$\div 10$), and \texttt{round\_number\_change} -- and keep a resulting
variant only when the perturbed value differs from the anchor. Because the
anchor's occurrence in the table is unique by construction, any different
value directly contradicts the one grounded fact that made the original
claim true, so the perturbed claim's label is \textsc{false} by
construction; this substitutes, for our controlled construction setting,
the ``checking against the table'' verification step a manual annotator
would otherwise perform.

This construction is deliberately restricted to claims with a groundable
anchor: a claim whose truth depends on a derived quantity that is neither a
raw cell nor one of our computed aggregates (e.g.\ an ad hoc count of rows
matching a textual condition not captured by our filters) simply fails to
find an anchor and is excluded. This is a limitation, not a bug -- it means
NumNear-TabFact is biased towards the lookup/count/aggregation claims that
are literally groundable, which is exactly the numeric regime this paper
targets (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data.} We use TabFact \cite{chen2020tabfact}: all claims in the test
split matching a numeric-claim heuristic (contains a digit or number word,
or a count/comparison/superlative/aggregation cue phrase) are used for
evaluation, with no sampling -- $11{,}400$ claims, exact count. Claims
are tagged into \{lookup, count, comparison, superlative, aggregation,
other\} by documented keyword heuristics
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses their imperfection). The full
evaluation set (original test claims plus their test-derived NumNear-TabFact
near-misses, $16{,}874$ claims total) is deterministically shuffled (seed
42) and split in half by claim: the first half is evaluated with the local
model, the second with the hosted model (below), so that both runs draw from
the same underlying distribution rather than from hand-picked subsets. Each
half retains TabFact's own calibration/test assignment, stratified by
(label, claim type).

\textbf{Models.} Two models, evaluated on disjoint claim halves as described
above, via the same provider-agnostic client (Section~\ref{sec:method}
notes this requires no code changes to add a model): \textbf{Gemini 3.5
Flash-Lite} (hosted, paid tier; $8{,}205$ of $8{,}437$ assigned claims
completed before the evaluation budget was exhausted -- $97.3\%$) as the
primary result, and \textbf{Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct} (run locally via Ollama,
chosen initially because free-tier hosted access proved too rate-limited for
this project's scale -- Section~\ref{sec:limitations}; $3{,}752$ of $8{,}437$
claims completed at submission time, reported as an ongoing secondary
comparison) as a lower-capability reference point. $K=5$ sampled checks at
temperature $0.7$ for our method, greedy ($K=1$) for B3, for both models.

\textbf{Metrics.} Accuracy and macro-F1 on original vs.\ near-miss claims;
risk--coverage curves for all three confidence signals
(Section~\ref{sec:cross-method}); realised conformal risk and coverage for
$\alpha\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.30\}$ -- widened beyond the three
target rates in our original design once calibration on $\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$
alone revealed a hard floor (Section~\ref{sec:results}), to characterise
where each signal actually becomes usable; per-claim-type breakdown;
McNemar's test between methods; 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals on
headline metrics; token cost and latency per claim.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\input{tables/main_results.tex}

\textbf{Accuracy.} On original claims, the check-execution methods are
competitive with the free-text baselines -- Ours reaches $70.1\%$ vs.\
B1's $76.1\%$ and B2's $74.0\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:main-results}; 95\%
bootstrap CIs in the accompanying CSV are all within $\pm 1.8$ points).
McNemar's test confirms B1 and B2 each significantly outperform Ours on
original claims ($\chi^2=28.5$, $p=9.5\times10^{-8}$ vs.\ B1;
$\chi^2=13.0$, $p=3.1\times10^{-4}$ vs.\ B2), while Ours significantly
outperforms the single-check baseline B3 ($\chi^2=22.3$,
$p=2.3\times10^{-6}$), confirming that sampling $K=5$ checks and taking a
majority vote is a real improvement over a single greedy check, even though
neither closes the gap to free-text verdicts on this model. On near-miss
claims, B3 and Ours drop sharply (to $43.5\%$ and $46.8\%$) while B1 and B2
\emph{rise} (to $78.1\%$ and $82.4\%$) -- Section~\ref{sec:error-analysis}
shows this is not near-miss detection so much as a shift toward predicting
\textsc{false} that happens to align with near-miss claims being false by
construction, and that this shift itself tracks perturbation difficulty in
an interpretable way.

\label{sec:cross-model}
\textbf{Cross-model comparison.} We also compare the
hosted and local models directly (full per-method numbers in
\texttt{results/tables/cross\_model\_comparison.csv}).
B1/B2 degrade gracefully moving to the smaller model ($76.1\%\to74.3\%$,
$76.4\%\to73.2\%$), but B3 and Ours degrade sharply
($58.5\%\to31.4\%$, $62.0\%\to42.6\%$), with the local model's
\textsc{unverifiable} rate rising to $50.3\%$ for B3 and $32.0\%$ for Ours
(vs.\ $11.8\%$/$5.7\%$ hosted). This is the clearest evidence in our results
that execution-agreement's value is conditional on the base model's
reliability at the check-generation sub-task specifically, not on general
language understanding (which B1/B2 also require and which degrades far
less).

\textbf{Risk--coverage.} This is the central result. We calibrate all three
confidence signals from Section~\ref{sec:cross-method} independently and
report realised risk and coverage on the disjoint test half
(Table~\ref{tab:risk-coverage}, Figure~\ref{fig:risk-coverage}).
\emph{Within-method} execution-agreement
($s$) certifies \emph{none} of $\alpha\in\{0.02,\ldots,0.30\}$ with non-zero
coverage: its realised risk floors at $29.5\%$ even when restricted to the
single most confident bucket (full 5/5 agreement, $67\%$ of test claims),
because $K=5$ only yields six distinguishable confidence levels, and the
finite-sample correction (Section~\ref{sec:method}) correctly refuses to
certify a rate the data cannot support -- it abstains on everything rather
than overstate the guarantee. B2's self-reported confidence does better but
is saturated ($88\%$ of claims report confidence $=100$) and only certifies
$\alpha\ge0.15$, at $88.2\%$ coverage. The \emph{cross-method} signal
$s_\times$ is the only one of the three that certifies a strict target:
$\alpha=0.10$ at $43.9\%$ coverage, and $\alpha\ge0.15$ at $56.3\%$
coverage (full risk/coverage grid in Table~\ref{tab:risk-coverage}; the
accuracy-by-confidence-bucket breakdown underlying this discussion is in
\texttt{results/tables/gemini\_accuracy\_by\_confidence.csv}). We read this

\section{Error Analysis}
\label{sec:error-analysis}

\input{tables/error_analysis_table.tex}

We sample 30 failures per method from the Gemini test set (923--842 total
failures per method; Table~\ref{tab:error-categories}) and categorise each
by inspecting its raw output (B1/B2) or executed check (B3/Ours). Two
findings stand out. First, \textbf{the dominant failure mode on a capable
model is semantic, not syntactic}: for both B3 and Ours, roughly
two-thirds of sampled failures are a \emph{genuine\_reasoning\_error} -- the
check parses and executes without any error, but encodes the wrong filter,
column, or operation for the claim -- versus a small remainder of
\emph{wrong\_column}, \emph{wrong\_filter}, and \emph{number\_parsing\_error}
cases. This is the opposite pattern from the local 7B model
(Section~\ref{sec:cross-model}), where malformed JSON dominated; it suggests
that a stronger model shifts the bottleneck from ``can it write a valid
check'' to ``can it write the \emph{right} check,'' which execution-agreement
by itself does not directly diagnose. Second, \textbf{B2's confidence format
increases its own failure rate}: over half of B2's sampled failures are a
\emph{format\_error} (the two-line \texttt{VERDICT}/\texttt{CONFIDENCE}
template not matching our parser) rather than a wrong verdict, versus only
$2/30$ for B1's single-word format -- a concrete, fixable cost of asking for
structured self-reported confidence that a production deployment should
account for (e.g.\ with a more lenient parser or a structured-output API
feature) rather than treating as a reasoning failure. Third, the near-miss
behavioural shift noted in Section~\ref{sec:results} tracks perturbation
subtlety closely: B1's accuracy on near-miss claims (equivalently, its rate
of predicting \textsc{false}, since every near-miss claim is false by
construction) is $70.5\%$ for off-by-one edits (the smallest possible
change) vs.\ $76.6\%$ for round-number changes, $78.7\%$ for magnitude
changes, and $89.7\%$ for digit-swaps (which can produce a very
different-looking number) -- a graded, interpretable pattern, not flat bias
toward one label.

\section{Deployment: RPA Escalation Workflow}
\label{sec:deployment}

Every abstained claim is written -- with its agreement score, executed
checks, and abstention reason -- to a review queue. A UiPath REFramework
automation drives the process: a Dispatcher validates each claim (rejecting
a missing table, empty claim, or duplicate ID as a business exception before
spending an LLM call) and enqueues the rest to an Orchestrator queue; a
Performer calls a FastAPI service (\texttt{POST /verify}) wrapped in a Retry
Scope that retries only system exceptions (LLM/API unavailability, HTTP 503),
never business exceptions (malformed input, HTTP 400/422); abstained claims
generate a review ticket and email notification, with overdue tickets
escalated on a schedule. Queue population is exactly the set of claims below
$\lambda_\alpha$, so reviewer workload is a direct, quantified consequence of
$\alpha$, not a separately tuned rule.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Within-method agreement's coarseness.} $K=5$ gives only $K{+}1=6$
distinguishable agreement levels, and even unanimous agreement has a
realised error rate ($29.5\%$) above every $\alpha$ tested up to $0.30$ --
the procedure correctly refuses to certify these rates rather than overstate
the guarantee, but within-method agreement alone cannot support useful risk
control here. The cross-method signal (Section~\ref{sec:cross-method})
does certify $\alpha=0.10$, but departs from the original single-signal
design; raw accuracy improved only modestly from $K{=}1$ to $5$
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations}), so closing this gap likely needs a
qualitatively different or continuous-valued signal, not simply a larger
$K$.
\textbf{Model capability and coverage.} Section~\ref{sec:cross-model}'s
comparison is partial: $3{,}752/8{,}437$ local-model claims (ongoing) and
$8{,}205/8{,}437$ ($97.3\%$) hosted-model claims (budget exhausted), so both
indicate the qualitative pattern rather than final complete-corpus numbers.
We evaluate exactly two models through a provider-agnostic interface
(Section~\ref{sec:method}) that makes a third model or either run's
completion a configuration change, not new code.
\textbf{English Wikipedia tables and FEVEROUS.} TabFact's tables are
English-language Wikipedia infoboxes; generalisation to other domains,
languages, or noisier institutional spreadsheets is untested at scale.
FEVEROUS was planned as a second dataset, but its Wikipedia evidence dump is
too large to responsibly process within this project's timeline and remains
future work. \textbf{Heuristic claim typing.} The numeric-claim filter and
lookup/count/comparison/superlative/aggregation typing are keyword
heuristics, not a semantic parse, and will mis-tag unlisted phrasing.
\textbf{NumNear-TabFact's groundability restriction.} Near-miss construction
(Section~\ref{sec:nearmiss}) only covers claims with a literal or aggregate
anchor; claims depending on an unanchored derived quantity are excluded by
construction. \textbf{The exchangeability assumption.} The conformal
guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}) assumes calibration and test
claims are exchangeable; a post-calibration distribution shift (e.g.\ new
table schemas each term) is not covered and would need re-calibration.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented Certified Numeric Verification: executable, sampled checks in
place of free-text LLM arithmetic, calibrated via split-conformal risk
control, with every sub-threshold claim routed to an auditable human-review
workflow. NumNear-TabFact's measured $0.961$ similarity shows the numeric
near-miss problem is not a corner case. On $8{,}205$ Gemini-evaluated
claims, within-method execution-agreement correlates with correctness but is
too coarse-grained to certify strict error rates
($\alpha\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$) at useful coverage -- reported rather than
obscured, since correctly abstaining is itself evidence the calibration
machinery works. A cross-method signal (execution-based and free-text
verdicts agreeing) recovers a genuine result, certifying $\alpha=0.10$ at
$43.9\%$ coverage; a secondary local-model comparison shows the same pattern
at lower reliability, so practical value scales with base-model capability
rather than being fixed by the method. Future work: completing both runs to
full corpus, FEVEROUS and other domains, learned claim typing, and a
confidence signal -- larger $K$, more independent methods, or a continuous
score -- that closes the within- vs.\ cross-method gap. This targets SDG 16
(auditable numeric claims over institutional data) and SDG 4 (an
institutional deployment over educational records) \cite{un2015sdgs}.
